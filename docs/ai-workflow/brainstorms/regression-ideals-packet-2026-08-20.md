**51 — Glute bridge form.** Heels close enough to brush your fingertips, feet flat, ribs down. Drive through the heels and squeeze the glutes to lift — the cue is "push the floor away," not "arch up." Stop when your body's a straight line shoulder-to-knee; higher than that is your lower back taking over. One-second squeeze at the top, control down. If you feel it mostly in your hamstrings, walk the heels in; mostly lower back, reset the ribs and tuck slightly. Exhale up.

**52 — 5k warm-up.** Five to ten minutes, progressive: start with three or four minutes of easy jogging, then dynamic work — leg swings, walking lunges, high knees, butt kicks, twenty to thirty meters each — and finish with two or three relaxed strides building to around race effort. Save long static stretching for after the run; before it, you want the muscles warm and springy, not lengthened and sleepy.

**53 — Water on training days.** Skip the one-size formulas — anchor on two things: urine the color of pale straw across the day, and roughly an extra half-liter around the session itself, more in heat or if you're a heavy sweater. Sip through the day rather than chugging before training; a slosh in your stomach on the first set means you did it in the wrong order.

**54 — DOMS vs injury.** DOMS lives in the muscle belly, shows up both sides if you trained both sides, peaks a day or two later, and warms UP as you move. Injury is usually one-sided, sharper, often has a moment you can point to ("on that third rep"), sits at or near a joint, and gets worse with movement, not better. Swelling, instability, or pain that changes how you move — that's the injury column. When it's genuinely ambiguous, we treat it as the cautious option for a session and let the pattern declare itself.

**55 — What a deload is.** A planned easy week — same movements, roughly half the volume and comfortably lighter — so the fatigue you've been quietly stacking can drain while the adaptations you paid for actually consolidate. Training hard creates the stimulus; recovering is when you cash it in. It's monthly because fatigue accumulates invisibly: performance holds, holds, holds, then dips — the deload lands before the dip. Come back the week after and the weights feel lighter; that's the point.

**56 — Eating an hour before an evening session.** Something with easy carbs and a bit of protein, light on fat and fiber — those slow digestion and sit heavy under load. A banana with yogurt, toast with a couple of eggs, a small rice bowl. Enough to fuel, not enough to feel. If you're coming straight from work with ninety minutes' gap, a normal meal is fine; at an hour, keep it modest.

**57 — Small living-room setup.** Claim a two-by-two-meter patch and keep it permanently clear — friction kills home training. Mat down for floor work, dumbbells racked or cornered where you'll never trip on them, and check your overhead clearance with arms fully extended before any pressing. No dropping weights on a residential floor — control every rep down, which is better training anyway. If you can angle your phone against something at knee height, you've got a form camera.

**58 — Incline walking.** Genuinely useful, not folklore. It's low-impact steady work that burns real calories, costs you almost nothing in recovery, and you can do it the day after heavy legs without wrecking anything. What it isn't is magic — fat loss still comes from the overall deficit; incline walking is just one of the most sustainable ways to widen it. Ten to fifteen percent grade at a pace you could hold a conversation at is the sweet spot.

**59 — RDL vs deadlift.** The conventional deadlift starts from the floor every rep — you set up under the bar, break it off the ground, whole posterior chain plus a lot of legs. The Romanian starts standing and never touches down: soft knees, hips travel BACK, bar slides down the thighs to around mid-shin, then hips drive through. RDLs bias the hamstrings and glutes hard with a big stretch under load; conventionals let you move the most total weight. In programs they do different jobs — one builds the hinge, the other tests it.

**60 — Sleep on four training days.** Seven to nine hours like everyone else — but training four days a week pushes you toward the top of that range, because sleep is where the actual rebuilding happens. Consistency beats totals: same window every night outperforms two sixes and two tens. You'll see a short night in your training before you feel it — bar speed and appetite for hard sets drop first. A twenty-minute nap patches a bad night; it doesn't replace the pattern.
