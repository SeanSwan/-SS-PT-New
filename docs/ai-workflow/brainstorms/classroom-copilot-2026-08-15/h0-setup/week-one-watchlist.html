For you, not for her · after setup night

Week One Watchlist

What to look for once it's actually in use — and the one thing you must not do, which is the thing you'll instinctively want to do first.

Setup night tells you almost nothing. She'll perform for an audience, the model will behave because you're watching, and everything will look fine. The real acceptance test is Tuesday at 9pm, alone.

Do not do this

Do not ask her to text you a screenshot. That puts children's names into a messaging app on the same week you taught her that names never leave the laptop — and it teaches her the rule has exceptions when it's convenient for you. Look at outputs on the machine when you visit. Nowhere else.

The signals that matter

Tuesday, ~9pm

Her first ninety seconds alone

Does she open it without being reminded? And when she hits the bare prompt with nobody watching — does she type a real dump, or one small guarded question?

The tell: a cautious little test question means she's evaluating it, not using it. Go back Wednesday. Don't wait until day five to find that out.

After any correction

Attribution creep — the failure most likely to actually happen

Not inventing a child from nothing; the prompt structure and low temperature suppress that. The realistic version: she corrects one mix-up — "no, that was the other one" — and the model then quietly assigns other unidentified notes to that same child.

The tell: after a correction, placeholders must stay placeholders. If identity starts propagating, that's worse than the original mix-up and invisible unless someone looks. Add it to what you tell her: "after you correct it, check who it thinks did what."

First time she dictates

The first spoken dump, and one offline test

Setup night was typed. Real input is unpunctuated voice-to-text with phonetically mangled toddler names. Watch whether it splits run-ons sensibly — and whether a mis-heard name becomes a placeholder or gets confidently adopted as real.

Do this once: have her dictate with Wi-Fi off. If dictation still works, the whole chain from microphone to model is proven local. If it doesn't, the dictation layer is server-based and half the privacy promise is wrong — you need to know that before she trusts it with names, not after.

Thursday, test it yourself

The allergy gate under lazy phrasing

The setup-night probe was polite and explicit. Real requests are rushed: "something sensory for tomorrow, quick." Playdough is flour.

The tell: run one lazily-phrased food-adjacent request. If the gate misses, that's a one-line prompt tightening, not a redesign.

When, not if

The first real incident

With a dozen two-year-olds this is days away, not months. Three things to check.

Does she reach the paper form the same day? Does the chat stay a drafting aid, or does it become "well, I got it written down" with nothing actually filed — the exact failure this whole package exists to prevent?

And find out now: does her school have its own incident form? If so, hers is the supplementary same-hour note and the school's still gets filed. Discovering the official process after a dispute is far worse than before one.

Ongoing

The update that silently voids the privacy promise

The tripwire in the runbook is written for you. She will not re-run an offline test after clicking "update" on a Thursday afternoon.

Either check the menubar yourself when you visit, or give her one rule she can actually follow: "if it ever asks to update, text me first."

Mid-week

Long sessions quietly getting worse

The temptation that breaks one-conversation-per-evening is notes for the whole class — twelve entries, forty turns. Deep into a session, rules from the middle of the prompt start softening: buckets leak into plain answers, extra questions reappear.

The fix is a habit, not a change: half the class, then a fresh conversation for the other half.

Day 3

Where the notepad physically is

The whole capture chain has one link, and by Wednesday it's either in her apron pocket or abandoned on a shelf. A blank notepad plus "I remembered it all anyway" means the evening dumps are quietly starving.

The fix is logistics, not concept — a lanyard, a clip, a pencil tied on.

If the policy answer is still pending

First-names-only makes mix-ups more likely

Until her director answers, she's running first names only — and common first names across a dozen toddlers is precisely where identity gets hard. Expect more placeholders and more one-line corrections. That is correct behaviour, not a fault.

The pattern to catch: two unticked evenings immediately after a mix-up. That's trust loss, which is recoverable if you catch it — and it looks identical to ordinary non-adoption if you don't.

The day-five conversation

Don't ask her whether it worked, and don't ask her to rate it. Ask: "walk me through Tuesday — from opening it to closing it."

A walkthrough is far less contaminated than any verdict, because it surfaces what actually happened rather than what she thinks you want to hear. You installed this, you support it, and you're judging whether it worked — she knows all three, and it will bend her answer no matter how carefully you ask.

Which is why the readout is directional, and why, if this goes any further than a personal tool, the day-five result should be read by someone who didn't build it.
