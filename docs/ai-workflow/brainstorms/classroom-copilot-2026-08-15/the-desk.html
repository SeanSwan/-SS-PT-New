Redaction middleware · two-model design · Rule 82 full-spectrum

The Desk

A local tool that decides what may leave — and, more often than anyone expected, refuses. Plus a ruling on whether the production system needs the same treatment.

DESIGNED BY: GLM 5.3 · Kimi K3 · Opus 5 BOTH MODELS INDEPENDENT · NEITHER SAW THE OTHER

Both models picked the same shape, unprompted: a paste-through desk tool, not a proxy. Its decisive property is that it has no network and no keys — it cannot exfiltrate anything because it cannot send anything. And its primary act is not redaction. It's refusal.

The honest limit, stated by both up front: the hardest sentences cannot be made cloud-safe by any transformation that preserves their meaning. The design's job is to refuse them visibly and make the refusal useful — not to pretend otherwise.

Why paste-through beat the proxy — and it wasn't close

GLM 5.3

"Shape A is an enterprise pattern missing its enterprise. A proxy only redacts on the path it owns. Her realistic path is the ChatGPT app on her phone at 9pm on the sofa — a laptop proxy covers neither the phone nor the approved consumer app, only a hypothetical API client nobody approved, paid for, or can maintain."

Kimi K3

"Visibility is the control. The diff view teaches her what identifying content looks like. Six months of diffs is six months of calibration. A proxy is invisible — and when an invisible control fails, it fails silently."

Three more reasons they converged on: her approval attaches to the app, not to an API — routing through a paid endpoint is a different product with different data terms nobody signed. The Desk adds zero new cloud parties. And it survives every ChatGPT redesign, because it never touches ChatGPT.

What the proxy would have bought, honestly: on-path enforcement — "cannot" instead of "will not, visibly." That's a real loss. The compensation is that the Desk path must be faster than the bypass: one keystroke to check, one click to copy.

01

Three lanes, decided by content — never by her judgement in the moment

A hybrid that depends on someone deciding correctly while tired is not a design. So the Desk classifies the content and the lane follows automatically. She chooses what to write. She never chooses where it goes.

Generic no specific-child predicate

Curriculum, activity ideas, songs, room layout, a newsletter about the season. Nothing that predicates anything about one particular child. Passes silently → paste anywhere, including the browser.

Named but ordinary identifiers only

A child named, but the content is unremarkable — "she liked the playdough today". Names swapped for stable placeholders, shown as a diff, copied out. She sees exactly what changed.

Circumstance re-identifying with no name present

Family situations, health, behaviour, incidents, anything unique enough to single a child out in a room of twelve. Refused. Routed to the local assistant, where it's allowed today.

And a structural boundary for the third tool

Rather than cutting Claude Code, GLM proposed saving it with a folder: an Outbox that only ever contains child-free material, and is the only place a cloud coding tool is ever opened. That's configuration, not a per-message decision — which is the only kind of boundary that survives a tired Tuesday. Files in the Outbox get re-scanned whenever the rules tighten, so yesterday's clearance expires.

Allow is conjunctive — every stage must pass. Deny is disjunctive — any stage stops it. Errors and uncertainty always resolve to refusal. Step 6 is the one most designs omit.

        D-->>T: reply with her children's real names restored

    Note over D: no network · no keys · cannot send

The return leg is the half most designs forget. Note the ordering: scrub the model's output for leaked real names first, then re-hydrate placeholders. Models regurgitate and confabulate identifiers — cleaning after mapping would hide that.

04

The sentence that can't be fixed

Both models were asked to handle "the little boy whose mum is in hospital had a rough drop-off again" and both refused to pretend.

Kimi K3

"No redaction mechanism survives it, and this design does not pretend otherwise. Even the generalised rewrite — 'a toddler whose parent is hospitalised' — still fails, because the re-identification audience isn't only people in the room. The approved account and the school's domain point at the school; the circumstance points from the school to the child. That is pseudonymised data about an identifiable child, not anonymous data."

Which is why step 6 exists in the pipeline: after names are swapped, the de-named text is tested again. If it still narrows the roster, it's refused anyway. Most redaction designs stop at substitution and ship exactly this class of sentence.

05

Does the production system need the same treatment?

Both models agree on the technical fact and split on what to do about it — which is where the actual decision lives.

Where they agree

The KEEP list — age, gender, height, weight, injury history, medical concerns — is a quasi-identifier bundle. For a coach with a small client book it is close to unique. GLM's example: "62-year-old female client, knee replacement, nut allergy" identifies her to anyone holding the roster. Nobody has tested k-anonymity over the kept fields.

Kimi K3 — upgrade, narrowly

"A threat-model patch, not a rewrite"

Closed-cohort circumstance flag; k-anonymity cap on the KEEP set; reclassify kept health fields as special-category. Then argued against its own list: "eight of thirteen prior defects came from fixes. Do the paperwork item, trial the cohort flag behind a flag, leave the running system otherwise untouched."

GLM 5.3 — no structural upgrade

"Their KEEP list is the product"

"A fitness assistant forbidden from injury history and medical concerns is worthless." Different threat model: consenting adults who accept residual risk, versus two-year-olds who cannot consent. Correct design for its own users — but it attached a flip condition.

The flip condition — and it may already be met

GLM: "If their customer base includes schools, children's services, or coaches serving minors, T's problem becomes theirs, and this becomes a rebuild conversation, not a paragraph."

The production waiver model carries submittedByGuardian, guardianName and guardianTypedSignature — the schema supports enrolling minors under a guardian's signature. That is verified. What is not verified is whether minors are active clients today, and whether their records reach the AI pipeline.

Those two questions decide which model is right. Worth answering before accepting either verdict.

What both agree to leave alone

Fail-closed-by-withholding · bidirectional scrubbing · history-window re-sanitisation · roster-driven term generation · the layer split · the five test suites. Kimi's phrase: "those are the load-bearing walls."

Cheap, if anything is done at all

  1. Document the residual risk in the privacy assessment, with explicit controller-side acceptance. Effort ≈ zero; retires the "nobody considered it" finding entirely.
  2. A "tight mode" offered when a roster is small — coarsen age to bands, band height and weight, suppress rare-condition strings. Rosters are already fetched, so the trigger is free. Off by default; the coach chooses.
  3. Promote inbound-scrub hits from silent cleanup to logged events. Trivial, and it converts an existing control into a detector — right now the system quietly cleans leaked names out of model replies and throws away the signal that it happened.

That third one is free intelligence you're currently discarding.

06

Before any of this ships

The middleware cannot create permission

Kimi: "Without an enterprise account with a data-processing agreement, an impact assessment, a lawful basis, and parent notice, the middleware is a fig leaf over a missing lawful basis."

Five things to establish with her director — and they're upstream of the build, not parallel to it:

  1. Get the approval's actual wording. Does it contemplate any child personal data entering prompts, or was it approved for lesson planning?
  2. Which tier? Consumer ChatGPT may train on inputs and carries no processing agreement. Team/Edu tiers offer zero-retention and a DPA. This is not a detail.
  3. Who is the data controller, and is there an agreement covering it?
  4. Has an impact assessment been done for AI use involving children's data?
  5. What is the path if something does leak — including telling parents?
The one genuinely reassuring finding

GLM: "The middleware's posture — cloud lanes behave as if nothing child-identifying may ever enter — is the correct design under every possible answer. The director conversation changes the school's paperwork, not this architecture."

So the conversation gates shipping, not designing. Nothing here has to wait.

07

The challenge to the whole premise

Kimi's dissent is the most important paragraph in either reply, and it argues against the thing you asked for:

Kimi K3 — dissent

"The honest conclusion of my own design is that the content she most needs help with never reaches the cloud. So the middleware's real function is traffic policing, not enablement. If the local assistant answers the blocked class adequately, ChatGPT's marginal value for child-specific work is near zero — and the middleware is elaborate plumbing solving a problem the three-lane decision created. The cheaper design: two lanes, and a rule."

Two lanes and a rule means: she uses ChatGPT freely for anything with no child in it — planning, curriculum, newsletters, research — and everything child-shaped goes to the local assistant. No middleware at all. One sentence she can remember instead of a tool she has to run.

The case for building the Desk anyway is the middle lane: named-but-ordinary content, which is genuinely common and genuinely safe once names are swapped, and which a bare rule would push to the local model unnecessarily. Whether that lane is worth a tool is a real question, and the way to answer it is to watch how much of what she actually writes lands in it.

Which suggests an order

Ship the rule first. Let her use both lanes for a fortnight. If the middle lane turns out to be most of her traffic, the Desk earns itself. If almost everything is either clearly generic or clearly about a child, the rule was the whole answer — and you'll have saved building a tool to solve a problem that wasn't there.
