System architecture · companion to the Classroom Copilot plan

The Switchyard

How a local assistant decides which brain to use, and what is never allowed to leave the building — designed by a six-model panel that spent most of its effort attacking it.

PANEL: Kimi K3 · GLM 5.3 · HY3 · Opus 5 TIERS: her 8B · her 14B · your 5090 — no vendor in v1 APPLIES TO: her classroom assistant AND your own operator bridge

Two axes decide every request: what it touches and how hard it is. Sensitivity always outranks capability — you never reach for a cloud model to get a better answer about a child, you reach for a bigger local one. And the boundary that enforces it must be installed once by you, not maintained by a teacher.

The panel's most useful contribution was not the design. It was three honest limits on what this can ever claim, in §06.

This is the layer underneath the classroom app — and it is genuinely dual-use. The same gateway that keeps a two-year-old's developmental notes off a vendor's servers is the one that keeps your family and immigration matters off them too. Build it once.

01

The routing matrix

Every request gets classified on two independent axes before anything runs.

Trivial workHard / creative work
Touches a person Small local model. Sorting a dump, tagging, extracting fields. Fast, cheap, constrained output. Big local model. Conference narratives, incident phrasing, difficult-message drafting. Never leaves the machine no matter how hard it is.
No personal data Small local model. Formatting, lists, rewrites. No reason to pay or wait. Your 5090. Lesson planning, activity ideas, curriculum research, long-form drafting — at 70B quality with no third party anywhere in the path.
The rule that makes it safe

Sensitivity outranks capability, always. "This is hard" is never a reason to escalate outward — it is a reason to escalate upward, locally. That single ordering is what makes the high-RAM laptop load-bearing rather than luxury: it means "I need a smarter answer about this child" has a good answer that never leaves the house.

Provenance is checked before content. Text from the notes store is blocked by origin — no classifier gets a vote on it, and no amount of difficulty routes it outward.

Three tiers — and no vendor at all

Adding your desktop displaces the vendor rather than the local model, and once it does, the vendor turns out to be barely reachable. So v1 ships with no third party in the architecture whatsoever.

That is not a cosmetic simplification. The vendor path is what forced the hardest parts of this design — semantic re-identification on outbound text, the pseudonym question, zero-retention contract terms, auditing a vendor's billing records against your own logs. All of it existed because someone else was on the far end. Remove the vendor and the gateway's job shrinks to gating one encrypted link to a machine you own, which is dramatically easier to build correctly and to install in an evening.

Why child data stops at T2

The school authorised her to hold those children's records — not you. Encryption doesn't change that: your desktop is hardware a person outside the authorised circle controls and could read. Legally it sits in the same category as a vendor, with a friendlier face. "Our teacher sends children's developmental notes to her partner's home computer" reads badly to a director, a parent, or a licensor regardless of how good the crypto is.

This is a judgement call and it's yours to make — but make it deliberately, not by default, and if you overrule it, do it knowing that's the sentence you'd be defending.

T3 stays up — but still degrades

The desktop is kept on deliberately, so T3 is the normal path for hard child-free work rather than an occasional bonus. Two reasons to keep the fallback anyway: Wake-on-LAN is dead on that machine (the BIOS route was exhausted and shelved), so nothing wakes it remotely — and "stays on" is an intention, while reboots, updates and power cuts are events. If T3 doesn't answer, the request quietly drops to her 14B and she never sees an error. It costs nothing and it prevents one genuinely bad midday.

Transport: an encrypted peer-to-peer mesh (Tailscale/WireGuard shape) — no open ports, no port-forwarding, nothing exposed to the internet. Worth checking early whether her school network permits it; if it doesn't, T3 simply never fires from work and the fallback already covers that case.

02

Hostile review, ported

Your standing discipline transfers, with one constraint that forces a fork: hostile review of personal-data work must itself stay local. You cannot hand a child's observation to a cheap cloud reviewer to critique.

WorkReviewed byLooking for
Family notes, conference narrativesLocal 14B, adversarial promptWrong child, invented detail, tone, anything not supported by the source note
Incident recordsLocal, checking her words onlyMissing fields, non-neutral language, absent witness or notification time. It never writes the account.
Lesson plans, activity sets, researchCheapest capable cloud reasonerAge-appropriateness, safety, feasibility for one adult alone, missing prep
The trivial sorting passNothingReviewing this would double latency inside a 5-minute window for no gain

When to review — one testable question

You said it should probably always review. The version that keeps your intent without breaking her midday clock: review anything that produces an artifact she acts on or sends. That's a single question the router can answer — does this output leave her head and go somewhere real? — rather than a judgement call it will get wrong.

Never generate

The panel was unanimous and emphatic: no AI-written incident narratives. An incident record is a legally protective document, and a model that invents one plausible detail destroys its value precisely when she needs it. She writes it; the machine only checks that she filled in the fields she'll forget at 10:40 with a crying child — time, witness, who was notified, what was said.

03

The gateway

One component every outbound request passes through. Its job is refusing, not scrubbing — because free text cannot be reliably de-identified. "The little boy whose mum is in hospital" contains no name and identifies a child completely.

Allow is conjunctive — every stage must pass. Deny is disjunctive — any stage can stop it. Uncertainty is always a block.

Why the classifier is deny-only

A local model judging whether text is safe is itself an inference step, and it can be wrong. Kimi's resolution is elegant: the classifier may veto but may never authorise. It can add a reason to refuse; it can never be the reason something was allowed. A wrong classifier then costs you utility, never secrecy.

Enforcement — and the correction that matters

The boundary can't be a convention the app agrees to honour. It has to hold when the app is buggy, compromised, or prompt-injected. That means: the app runs with no network route off the machine, and provider credentials live only in the gateway, in the OS keystore, readable by nothing else. The app talks to 127.0.0.1 with a dummy token. Even fully compromised, it has nowhere to send anything and nothing to authenticate with.

GLM's correction — take this seriously

"OS-level egress enforcement run by a preschool teacher is fantasy. The honest shippable form is a locked-down profile installed once by O — anything else quietly collapses 'assume the app is untrusted' into 'trust the app.'"

So: you install it, once, in person. She never configures a firewall. And because the most likely real-world failure is the boundary silently drifting — an OS update, a reinstall, both processes ending up under one account — a watchdog re-verifies the rule continuously and screams if it changed. Kimi was blunt that boundary drift, not a clever classifier miss, is the probable production failure.

Pseudonyms — narrow, or not at all

The panel split, and the strict reading wins for children. Substituting a token for a name is legitimate; substituting one for a circumstance is meaningless, because the circumstance is the identifier. Three rules if used at all:

  • Names only, never circumstances — and the de-named text gets re-tested: if it still narrows a 12-child roster, it's blocked anyway.
  • No stored mapping. Use a keyed function over the small closed roster, key in the OS keystore. There's no table to steal.
  • Task-scoped, never global. A stable token that co-occurs forty times with "hospital" and "custody" hands a vendor a dossier under a persistent key — and one future identity leak through any other channel retroactively deanonymises the whole accumulated history.

For minors' content specifically, Kimi's position is the safe one and I'd adopt it: block by class, never substitute.

04

What she actually sees

The failure to design against here is consent fatigue. A gate that asks permission for everything trains her to approve everything — which is worse than no gate, because it manufactures false confidence. So the friction is spent unevenly and deliberately.

Silent pass the large majority

No screen, no interruption, nothing logged but metadata. She never learns the gateway exists on these.

Shown once a small minority

A diff: what she wrote, and what would be sent. One glance, one button. Not a permission dialog — a content confirmation.

Refused rare, no override

Held on the device, explained calmly, and — critically — paired with a working local alternative. A refusal that dead-ends is what drives someone to a browser tab.

06

Three limits, stated honestly

The most valuable output of the panel. Each of these is a real hole; none is fixable by better engineering alone.

1 · The browser tab voids the claim

Kimi K3

"The humans can and will open a vendor's chat in a browser. That channel is outside every metric proposed and cannot be verified. The honest title is assistant egress guard, not privacy boundary. Treating the control as total is a false proof."

So don't call it a privacy boundary. And note what actually reduces this risk: it is not a stricter gate — a stricter gate increases it. It's making the local path good enough that she never wants the browser. The high-RAM laptop is the anti-shadow-IT mechanism. That is the real reason it matters.

2 · The local model can launder the data

GLM 5.3

"The gateway polices the door while the crown jewel walks out rewritten. A prompt-injected local model with tool access can launder tainted notes into clean-sounding prose before egress. This is the weakest joint in my own design."

The gateway inspects text at the door. If the local assistant has already paraphrased a child's note into generic-sounding prose, the door sees something clean. The fix isn't at the gateway — taint has to travel with the data through every local transformation, enforced at the store and tool layer. That is genuinely a separate design problem and I'd scope it as its own slice rather than pretend this design covers it.

3 · "Proof" is the wrong standard

GLM 5.3

"Proof requires bounding the adversary's auxiliary information, which is impossible by construction. Chasing proof yields theatre metrics; substitute measured failure rate under adaptive attack, and say so."

Report what's true: a measured miss-rate against a corpus of known attacks, plus canary values that must never appear outbound, plus a coverage check — the vendor's own usage records must match the gateway's log exactly. If the vendor billed for calls your gateway never saw, something bypassed it. That comparison is the single most useful ongoing assurance signal, and it costs nothing.

Zero vendors, not one

GLM argued for starting with a single vendor rather than several, since each additional provider multiplies audit surface and correlation risk for no early benefit. The 5090 takes that argument one step further and lands somewhere better: start with none. Every vendor-shaped problem — retention terms, billing-record audits, outbound re-identification, pseudonym leakage — is deferred rather than solved, and deferred problems cost nothing to maintain.

If a real gap shows up in use, add exactly one, on zero-retention terms, and re-open §03's harder machinery at that point. Not before.
