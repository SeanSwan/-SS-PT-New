Build plan · six-model panel · 15 Aug 2026

Classroom Copilot

A private, on-device assistant for one preschool teacher working a room of two-year-olds without an aide — and the honest case for not building most of it.

PANEL: Kimi K3 · GLM 5.3 · HY3 · Gemini 3.1 Pro · Fable 5 · Opus 5 DEVICES: Galaxy S24-class Android · MacBook Air, 24–32GB

Build nothing this weekend. Set up a local AI on her Mac, print a paper triage sheet, and add one phone shortcut. Then watch for five school days. If she uses it unprompted on four of them, build the app in ten working days. If she doesn't, the app was never the answer and you just saved a month.

All six models converged on this independently. Not one recommended shipping an app first.

The transcript you were sent isn't a request for an app. It's a specification written by accident — it already names the entire domain model and, more valuably, the interaction model: she talks in a messy paragraph, and something sorts it. That loop is the product. Everything else is furniture.

Six models reviewed a sanitized version of it. They agreed on more than I expected and split on five points, which Fable settled as Final Decider. Two of their findings overturn assumptions in my own brief.

01

The two findings that changed the design

The clock was wrong, and it would have killed the product

My brief anchored the daily loop to an "after-school reset." GLM caught that her family notes are due before pickup — so a dump that happens after school delivers its payoff after the deadline it exists to serve. She'd do the work, feel no relief, and quit inside three weeks.

The real anchor is the nap window — roughly the ninety minutes when a preschool teacher's hands are free for the only time all day. Everything moves there. The whole loop, dump through drafted notes, has to finish in under five minutes on a phone. That is now a hard latency budget that constrains the model choice, not a nice-to-have.

Local-first solved one governance problem and quietly created another

This is Fable's ruling and it's the sharpest thing in the panel:

Fable 5 · Final Decider

Everyone solved "the developer must not become an operator" and declared privacy done. Nobody noticed the fix creates a shadow education record in an employee's personal Apple ID. Developmental observations on a dozen two-year-olds, accumulating for a year in her private iCloud, are invisible to the school, unreachable by a parental-access request, and walk out the door when she changes jobs — the exact governance failure we were worried about, relocated rather than removed.

Fable wrote that against an iPhone. It transfers to Android unchanged — swap the personal Apple ID for a personal Google account and every word still holds. The governance problem was never about which vendor; it was about a year of children's records living in an employee's private account where the school can't see them.

The fix is structural and it inverts a core assumption: the app is a scratchpad, not an archive. Anything a child's record actually needs flows into the school's mandated systems the same day — which makes the copy-to-parent-app bridge a compliance feature, not a convenience — and child-linked items expire by default. We designed for data to persist. The correct design is for it to expire.

…and expiry, taken literally, would have deleted her only protection

Round 2 caught a contradiction in the sentence above. If child items expire on a rolling window, the app deletes exactly the contemporaneous incident records that protect her — at roughly the horizon where an incident becomes a dispute. Both reviewers flagged it independently, and it isn't a detail to resolve later: append-only, never-expiring record types have to exist in the v1 schema, or adding them later is a storage-layer rewrite.

The amendment

Observations expire. Incidents and promoted evidence never do. Everything unpromoted still ages out, so the app never becomes a shadow archive — but one tap rescues a record-worthy item into her own encrypted store. Two cheap fields in the v1 schema (pinned, expiryExempt) cost about a day and are the entire insurance policy for conferences, assessments, and the day something goes wrong.

02

Where the panel agreed

Independently, without seeing each other's answers:

  • One feature only: messy paragraph in → typed records out → one-tap correction. Every other value in the product is a query over its output.
  • H0 is not an app. Unanimous, unprompted.
  • Expo, one platform only. The panel said iOS; her phone is Android, so it's Android — see §03. Either way: reject native-macOS forks outright.
  • Voice is a fallback, not the backbone. All four attacked this assumption hard, from different angles.
  • Grammar-constrained JSON output. Free-text JSON from a small model breaks parsing weekly.
  • A rules-based sorter ships in v1 — the app must be useful as a dumb notepad if the model never loads.
  • Roster fuzzy-matching is v1 or never. Name-garbling is the top-three failure path.
  • Backup goes through her own account, never yours.
  • A 1.5B–3B model is enough. Sorting is extraction, not reasoning.
  • Check her employer's data policy before building. If the center forbids personal-device notes, the architecture is moot.

Why voice loses

GLM 5.3

Toddler rooms run 85–95dB; she's at arm's length while verbally de-escalating; models trained on close, adult-directed speech garble child names precisely where accuracy matters most.

Kimi K3

Voice capture fails socially before it fails technically. She will not narrate observations about children in front of those children, or their parents.

HY3

Week one she tries dictation during cleanup, it mangles a note, she can't fix it, concludes the app is broken, and reverts to paper permanently.

So: text-first, using Gboard's own on-device voice typing — already on her phone, free, genuinely good, and already under her thumb. No custom speech engine in v1. Ship none of it.

Be the aide once, and leave a machine behind

Nothing gets built. Three things get set up:

  • Her own local AI on the MacBook — Ollama, one 14B-class model at Q4 (her machine has the headroom for it), one system prompt carrying the assistant persona from her own transcript, one notes folder. Then spend the evening being the aide with a cloud model on generic content only — first-week plan, activity bank, room labels, procedure scripts. Print it all.

Earned additions

Weekly reset with triage and a done digest. The learning-narrative drafter for her display boards — blocked until you know the school's developmental vocabulary. Lesson-plan export. Voice capture if she asks for it. A bigger model on the Mac.

Never: photos, analytics, notifications, multi-teacher, cloud sync of child data, a rewards system. Two-year-olds need physical token routines; that is not a software problem.

What happens when it guesses wrong

This is the make-or-break interaction. A wrong guess must cost exactly one tap — never typing, never a menu. Tapping a type chip cycles it; tapping a child chip opens a grid of coloured squares. Low-confidence guesses show a dashed border so she knows where to look. Corrections are stored and fed back as examples, so the sorter improves on her actual vocabulary.

Two hard rules. Nothing child-linked ever auto-files silently — a misattributed observation about a two-year-old that nobody catches is worse than no system at all. And unreviewed items still appear in Today: an accumulating review queue is a second inbox, which is a design failure, not a feature.

What Android changed

The panel assumed an iPhone, and one of its neater conclusions died with that assumption: the same build no longer runs on her Mac. An APK doesn't run on macOS, so the phone and the laptop are now two separate tools that never sync. That's a real loss, and it's the honest cost of the platform she actually owns.

Three things got better, though, and they more than pay for it:

  • The architecture argument is over. Kimi's strongest dissent was that SwiftUI plus Apple's on-device model framework would beat React Native. Neither exists on Android. Expo wins by default, and you write TypeScript.
  • Distribution stops being a risk. No developer account, no TestFlight, no review. You build an APK and install it on her phone. The panel's "day 11 dies in a setup screen" failure mode largely evaporates.
  • Android has a capture gesture iOS can't match. A persistent silent notification with inline reply — always in her shade, one tap, type, send, never opens the app. This is a first-class Android primitive with no iOS equivalent, and it's a better answer than anything the original panel designed.

How close is this to your stack?

You keep TypeScript, React, styled-components and your component instincts. You drop Express, Sequelize, Postgres and Render entirely — there is no server here, which is the compliance posture. You add Expo, expo-sqlite, and later llama.rn.

Inference, now that the hardware is known

A 2024 Samsung flagship carries 12GB of RAM and a Snapdragon 8 Gen 3 — more headroom than most iPhones of the same year. The sorter is extraction into a fixed label set, so it stays small; latency beats eloquence when she's watching a spinner during nap.

One hardware caveat worth knowing: these phones throttle GPU hard under sustained load. Our workload is one short extraction per dump, not sustained generation, so this shouldn't bite — but it's the thing to check first if inference gets mysteriously slow.

Her own assistant on the Mac — do this first

This is the piece you asked about, and after the panel it's clearly the highest-value thing you can do this weekend. It's also the only part that helps her Monday.

Mirror the posture of your own setup, not its scope: local model, fail-closed, no automatic cloud fallback. That single property is what makes it safe for her to type a child's name into it — which is exactly what a cloud chat assistant can't offer her, and the reason her current workaround has a privacy problem she probably hasn't considered.

  1. Install Ollama, pull one 14B-class model at Q4. One. Not a menu. Her machine has the headroom, and this tier is a real step up from the 8B class for the drafting work she'll lean on most.
  2. Write the system prompt from her own transcript. She already articulated the assistant she wants, in her own words — that document is the prompt. Don't improve it.
  3. One folder, one habit. Not a system she maintains.
  4. Tape the rule to the laptop: child names go in the local one, never a browser tab.
Why this matters more than the app

If the local assistant alone carries her through the first fortnight, you've solved her problem for the cost of one evening — and you'll know it from the gate rather than from guessing. If it doesn't, you'll know precisely which part failed, which is worth more than any amount of planning.

If she only ever gets three more slices

S2, S3, S4 — in that order.

S2 (two days) is the difference between a tool she uses and one she abandons in week five. The morale features are load-bearing, not decorative — she named one unprompted, which is the tell.

S3 is the only slice whose absence can harm her rather than merely fail to help. With a dozen two-year-olds the next incident is days away, not months. Every week without it is uninsured exposure.

S4 is the thing she called most important in her own words. A roadmap that ignores the user's stated number one has failed at listening, however clever its ordering.

One honest flag on that ordering

Her stated priority lands at slice four. That's defensible on risk grounds — morale and safety genuinely outrank it — but if S2 and S3 take three calendar weeks and she spends those weeks still attempting forty-seven things after work, we may lose her before her top priority ships. If week-two data shows triage-level distress, S4 jumps ahead of S3 and we accept that exposure knowingly, out loud, with her — not by default.

What "enterprise-grade" honestly means here

All three reviewers reached the same verdict independently: there is no multi-teacher path that survives the compliance posture, and the fork is exact — it's the first line of sync code. Before it you hold nothing and operate nothing. After it, even a "harmless" relay, you hold metadata, breach duties, and a school that never signed anything. It can't be retrofitted; end-to-end encryption bolted onto an architecture that assumed a server can read records is a rewrite.

There is exactly one posture-compatible expansion, and it isn't multi-teacher: multi-device for the same teacher, through her own cloud, under her own key. Three to five days whenever she wants her phone and laptop to share.

So for this product, enterprise-grade means reliability (the rules-only path works with no model, no network, no account, forever), recoverability (restores are drilled, not hoped for), trustworthiness (append-only incidents, open export formats), and longevity — if the developer disappears, the app and the data both keep working. Two cheap decisions in S1 keep that fork open and cost nothing: record identity is always locally generated, and keys always derive from her passphrase.

How this fails

  1. The habit never forms. She's in extended care until evening; the moment you designed for doesn't exist in her day. Mitigation: anchor to nap, and let the H0 gate prove the habit before you write code.
  2. One wrong child, once. The sorter swaps two children on a behavioural note, a parent hears the wrong story, and she reverts to paper permanently. Mitigation: nothing child-linked auto-files; confidence is visible.
  3. Correction fatigue. More than about three wrong guesses a day, at more than a tap each, and the review becomes a chore she skips. Mitigation: one tap, always; rules-first so the easy cases never reach the model.
  4. Silent model rot. Storage pressure evicts the weights or an OS update breaks the binding; sorting quietly degrades. She won't report an error — she'll just feel the app got worse. Mitigation: health check on launch, and a genuinely good rules path rather than a vestigial one.
  5. The phone and the Mac never meet. Android killed the shared-build story, so there are now two tools holding different things, and she may expect one to know about the other. Mitigation: don't build sync — give them different jobs and say so plainly. The phone captures and sorts; the Mac plans and drafts long-form. If she starts wanting them joined, that's an H2 conversation, not a v1 feature.
  6. Her employer says no. If the centre forbids child notes on personal devices, everything above is moot. Mitigation: a one-page "what this is and how to delete it" for her director, before week two.
Kill criteria — three, not one

Habit. End of week two: if she has drafted family notes from the app on fewer than three of five school days, stop building and interview her. No rescue feature — that's how a dead product eats another month.

Accuracy. Round 2's correction: the habit gate alone passes even if the sorter is quietly mangling one record in seven and she's stopped trusting it without being able to say why. A tool used daily and quietly wrong is worse than one that fails the gate. So: at least 90% of extracted records accepted without correction across a logged sample of twenty. Miss it and the rules engine becomes primary, with the model demoted to a suggestion layer — decided in week two, not month three.

Wrong child — the unrecoverable one. A note about one child sent to another child's parents is a trust-ender and possibly a reportable disclosure. Zero wrong-child fields in shipped drafts, full stop. Two, and the model path is dead for family notes permanently — rules only, forever.
